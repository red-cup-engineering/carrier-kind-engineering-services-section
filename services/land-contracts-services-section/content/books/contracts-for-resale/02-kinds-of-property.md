# The kinds of property

Every parcel on your lists is read as one kind of property. The kind decides
who will buy it, how we price the offer, and what you say on the call. You
filter your call queue by kind (`kinds=infill-lot`, for example).

## Infill lot

Vacant residential land inside a city, big enough to build on. In Duluth, a
standard lot is 25 by 125 feet; many older plats are full of them, and many sit
empty because their owners live elsewhere.

- **Who buys:** home builders, nonprofit housing developers, people who want to
  build their own house.
- **How we price:** the builder's top price less our fee, never above 70% of
  the county's land estimate. The first offer usually opens near half the
  estimate.
- **What matters to the buyer:** the neighborhood, how many standard lots the
  parcel holds, whether water and sewer are at the street, and whether it is
  flat enough to build on.

Duluth is loosening its zoning to allow narrower lots and more kinds of
housing, so more of these lots become buildable each year. The city also gives
some tax-forfeited lots to builders for free, so a lot we sell must be better
placed, bigger, already served, or available sooner than what the city offers.
Ask the builder which (chapter 3).

## Underbuilt site

Commercial or industrial land of a quarter acre or more where the buildings are
worth little compared with the land: gravel lots, surface parking, storage
yards. On 2026-09-26, Duluth had 847 of them on our list.

- **Who buys:** developers of larger projects, apartment builders, businesses
  expanding.
- **How we price:** like lots, from the buyer's price down. Fees are larger
  because the land is worth more.
- **Example:** ten acres of commercial land on a Duluth overlook, owned by a
  company in New York, with $26,251 in unpaid taxes and a projected forfeiture
  in 2029. The county values the land at $318,800.

## Rural acreage

Vacant land outside cities, most often forest. The northern counties are full
of it: Lake County alone had 5,426 rural parcels on our list.

- **Who buys:** hunters, people who want a cabin site, neighbors expanding, and
  land investors who resell land on payment plans.
- **How we price:** the way land investors price, at a fifth to a half of the
  county's value, because the buyers pay that way and the land sells slowly.
- **Example:** forty acres of rural vacant land in Lake County, held by a trust
  whose trustee lives in Tennessee, never recorded as sold. The county values it
  at $183,100. The first offer opens at $45,800.
- **How it is worked:** mostly by mail, with a blind offer (chapter 7), and by
  calling the owners who answer.

## Seasonal parcel

Lake and cabin land the county classes as seasonal recreational property. It
is often on or near water, which buyers pay for.

- **Who buys:** cabin buyers and cabin builders.
- **Example:** 38 acres of seasonal recreational land in a Lake County
  township, held by an estate, the owner of record in Illinois, county value
  $134,700.

## House the owner does not live in

A house or small rental (one to four units) whose owner has told the county it
is not their homestead. In Minneapolis there were 11,762 of them on our list.

- **Who buys:** landlords and renovators.
- **How we price:** from the county's total estimate, land and building, at
  half to 70%, and only after someone has seen the inside. Repairs decide the
  price, and the county does not know them.
- **Example:** a single-family house in Proctor, not the owner's homestead,
  owned since 2005 by someone living elsewhere in Minnesota, with $8,455 in
  unpaid taxes. The county values it at $181,800.
- **What is different:** a house has tenants, leases, and a condition. A
  residential seller must give the buyer a written disclosure of known
  problems before signing (Minnesota Statutes 513.55), and the purchase
  agreement says so.

## Forfeited parcel

Land whose owner did not pay taxes long enough that the state took title. Since
July 2024, Minnesota requires the county to sell every forfeited parcel at a
public auction, usually online, within six months. Duluth had 3,785 forfeited
parcels in the county's records on 2026-09-26.

You never call anyone about these, because no private owner can sell them. They
are a second supply line: the acquisitions lead watches the county's auction
lists and matches them to buyers (chapter 14).

## A note on counties

The lists come from the county's own parcel records. St. Louis County's records
are the richest we have: they show unpaid taxes and the year a parcel is
projected to forfeit. Other counties come from the state's compiled parcel
records, which show value, size, class and ownership but not unpaid taxes.
Some counties write their classes as numbers rather than names. For those, the
list can tell vacant land by its size and place, but cannot yet tell a house
from a business.
